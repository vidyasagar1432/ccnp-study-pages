Common Topologies: Star, Bus, Ring, and Mesh

The four foundational physical/logical designs. Know how each works, its domains, failure behavior, and where they still appear today.

Topology Star Bus Ring Mesh Fundamentals

1. Overview

When people say "topology", they mean the shape of the network. The four most common are star, bus, ring, and mesh. The key is to remember: a design that is physically one thing can be logically another. This article focuses on the classic forms, their domains, and failure modes.

2. Diagrams at a Glance

3. Star Topology

Every node connects to a central device (switch, hub, or wireless controller in modern terms). It is the topology of nearly all modern LANs.

AspectNotes
Physical / LogicalUsually physical star; logically a star (and per-VLAN a logical bus)
DevicesCentral switch/hub, UTP/fiber home runs
Collision domainWith a hub: one domain. With a switch: one per port
Broadcast domainOne unless VLANs are used
ProsEasy to add/move/change, easy troubleshooting, cable break affects only one node
ConsCentral device is a single point of failure, more cable than bus, central device capacity limits
Modern useCampus access, SOHO, most Ethernet LANs

4. Bus Topology

A single shared backbone cable connects all devices. Traffic is broadcast onto the backbone and all nodes see it (they accept only if addressed to them).

AspectNotes
Physical / LogicalBoth physical and logical bus (historically)
MediaCoax (10BASE2, 10BASE5), sometimes thin/thick ethernet
Collision domainOne shared domain
Broadcast domainOne
ProsCheap, simple for small networks, less cable than star
ConsSingle backbone break kills network; terminators required; collisions as nodes increase; hard to troubleshoot
Modern useEssentially obsolete; important for historical and collision-domain questions

Note: Taps, terminators, and CSMA/CD were all required for 10BASE2/5. A bad terminator alone could bring the whole segment down.

5. Ring Topology

Nodes form a closed loop, each connected to two neighbors. Traffic travels around the ring in one or both directions depending on the implementation.

AspectNotes
Physical / LogicalCan be physical ring; Token Ring was logical ring; SONET/SDH use rings
AccessToken passing (Token Ring/FDDI) or deterministic timing; some use dual rings
Collision domainTypically no collisions with token passing (deterministic)
Broadcast domainUsually one ring unless segmented
ProsPredictable latency, equal access, efficient under heavy load, can self-heal with dual rings
ConsOne break affects ring unless protected; adding/removing nodes can disrupt; more complex than star
Modern useMetro/optical rings (SONET/SDH), some legacy industrial; Token Ring is extinct

Protection: Dual-homing/dual rings (e.g., UPSR/BLSR) allow traffic to reroute within ~50ms on a fiber cut.

6. Mesh Topology

Nodes have multiple paths between them. A full mesh connects every node to every other; a partial mesh connects only some for resilience.

AspectNotes
Physical / LogicalEither; routing can create a logical mesh over a different physical layout
LinksFull mesh: n(n-1)/2. Partial mesh: as needed
Collision domainUsually one per point-to-point link
Broadcast domainDepends on L2/L3 — often per subnet/routed
ProsHigh redundancy, no single point of failure (with full/partial), can balance traffic, survives multiple failures
ConsExpensive and complex at scale, cable count explodes, harder to manage and troubleshoot
Modern useWANs, data center leaf-spine (Clos), DMVPN/SD-WAN overlays, critical interconnects

Example: 4 nodes full mesh = 6 links. 5 nodes = 10 links. The cost curve is the reason partial mesh dominates in production.

8. When Each One Appears Today

  • Star: 99% of wired LANs. Your home switch, office access layer, and most data centers use star/hierarchical star designs.
  • Bus: Rare in production. Still appears in exam questions about collision domains, CSMA/CD, and historical Ethernet.
  • Ring: Carrier networks (SONET/SDH rings), some SCADA/industrial, and legacy Token Ring environments. Not used for modern LAN access.
  • Mesh: WAN (MPLS/DMVPN), partial mesh between sites, leaf-spine Clos fabrics in data centers, and mission-critical links.

9. Common Exam Traps

  • "Bus topology uses a central hub." No. Bus uses a backbone cable; star uses a central device.
  • "A switch makes a star a mesh." No. Physically/star logically usually; switches don't create every-to-every links.
  • "Ring topologies always have collisions." No. Token passing is deterministic and collision-free.
  • "Full mesh is practical for large networks." No. Link count and cost make it impractical; use partial mesh or a fabric.
  • "A broken cable in a bus takes only one node down." No. It can break the entire segment.
  • "Star is more fault-tolerant than mesh." Usually false if central device has no redundancy; full mesh has no single point of failure.
  • "Physical mesh means logical mesh." Not necessarily; STP can block links and create a logical tree.

10. Key Takeaways

  • Star is the modern standard: easy, manageable, but depends on the central device.
  • Bus is cheap but fragile: one fault brings down the whole segment and collisions limit scale.
  • Ring is deterministic and self-healing with dual rings, but less flexible than star.
  • Mesh offers maximum resilience: use full mesh only for small n, else partial mesh or Clos fabrics.
  • Always distinguish physical vs logical: what you see cabled isn't always what traffic uses.
  • For domains: bus/ring historically shared; switch-based star gives per-port collision domains.
