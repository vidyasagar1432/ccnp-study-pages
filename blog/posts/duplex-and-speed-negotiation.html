Duplex and Speed Negotiation: Why Auto/Auto Matters

FLP bursts, parallel detection, and the half-duplex assumption that turns a simple mismatch into late collisions and mystery slowdowns.

Autonegotiation Duplex Speed Layer 1

1. Two Settings That Must Agree

Every copper Ethernet link has a speed and a duplex mode. Speed is the signalling rate — 10 Mb/s, 100 Mb/s, 1 Gb/s, 2.5 Gb/s, 5 Gb/s, 10 Gb/s. Duplex is the direction of transmission:

  • Half duplex — one direction at a time. The station must sense the carrier and use CSMA/CD, because the medium is shared.
  • Full duplex — transmit and receive at the same time on separate pairs. No carrier sense, no collisions.

The two settings are negotiated together, but they fail differently. A speed mismatch usually means no link at all: the receiver cannot decode a signal at the wrong rate. A duplex mismatch links up and passes traffic — badly. That is why duplex problems survive so long in production: the link light is green.

A speed mismatch is loud. A duplex mismatch is quiet, intermittent, and usually blamed on the application.

2. What Autonegotiation Actually Exchanges

Autonegotiation (IEEE 802.3 Clause 28, introduced with 100BASE-TX in 1995) lets two copper ports agree on the best common mode before passing data. It is not a single "what speed are you?" question. Each end sends a fast link pulse (FLP) burst: 33 pulses carrying a 16-bit link code word, repeated roughly every 16 ms.

The link code word advertises:

  • Selector field — which standard the capabilities belong to (IEEE 802.3).
  • Technology ability — supported 10/100 modes and full/half variants.
  • Pause capability — symmetric or asymmetric flow control.
  • Remote fault — the peer has detected a problem.
  • Next page — more capability data follows.

Both ends compare bitmaps and select the highest common capability: 1000BASE-T over 100BASE-TX, 100BASE-TX over 10BASE-T, and full duplex over half duplex for the same speed. Pause is resolved at the same time, but it is a separate feature from duplex.

Gigabit copper adds a timing problem. 1000BASE-T uses all four pairs simultaneously, so one PHY must be the clock master and the other the slave. That role is negotiated in next-page exchanges. This is why 1000BASE-T is not a "set it and forget it" mode: the master/slave agreement is part of autonegotiation, not an optional extra.

Fiber is different. 1000BASE-X has an optional Clause 37 negotiation that agrees duplex and pause but not speed — the optics fix the rate. 10GBASE-SR/LR links are normally fixed and do not autonegotiate at all. A copper 10GBASE-T port does negotiate, because it can run at multiple rates.

3. Parallel Detection: Where Mismatches Are Born

Both sides auto: a complete agreement. One side forced: speed is detected, duplex is guessed.

If both ends are left on auto, they exchange FLPs and agree on speed and duplex together. The trouble starts when one end is hard-coded. A forced port does not send FLP bursts; it simply drives its normal signal — link test pulses at 10 Mb/s, idle at 100 Mb/s, or its gigabit signalling.

The auto end falls back to parallel detection. It listens to the incoming signal, recognises the speed from the clock and encoding, and brings the link up. But a forced signal carries no capability information, so the auto end cannot know the peer's duplex. The standard says: assume half duplex.

That is the entire mechanism behind the classic mismatch:

  • Forced full + auto → the auto side comes up half, the forced side stays full. Mismatch.
  • Forced half + auto → the auto side assumes half, and both are half. The link matches, though it is still not a recommended design.

1000BASE-T changes the rules. Because master/slave must be negotiated, disabling autonegotiation can prevent the link from coming up at all, or leave it unstable. Hard-coding 1000/full on one side and auto on the other is not a reliable configuration; both ends should negotiate.

4. Anatomy of a Duplex Mismatch

Picture a link with one full-duplex end and one half-duplex end. The full side has no carrier sense: it transmits whenever it has a frame, even while the half side is transmitting. The half side still obeys CSMA/CD.

  1. The half side senses idle and starts sending a frame.
  2. The full side, unaware, starts sending at the same time.
  3. The half side detects a collision, sends a jam, and backs off. Its frame is destroyed.
  4. If the collision occurs within the first 512 bit times, the half side retries normally. If it occurs later, it is a late collision and the MAC does not retry; the upper layer must recover.
  5. The full side's frame is corrupted at the half side, but the full side has no way to know. TCP retransmits later, which looks like application slowness.

The counters are asymmetric, and that is the diagnostic signature:

Side Typical counters Why
Half duplexCollisions, late collisions, excessive collisions, deferredIt transmits into the full side's transmissions
Full duplexFCS/CRC errors, runts, alignment errorsIt receives fragments and jam from the half side
BothRetransmissions, low goodput, high latencyUpper layers recover from the loss

Traffic from the full side to the half side often looks fine as long as the half side stays quiet; traffic from the half side to the full side collides. That one-way asymmetry is why a ping or a small download can succeed while a backup or a file copy crawls. The link is not down, and it is not congested — it is half of a conversation talking over the other half.

5. Reading Both Ends

Never diagnose a duplex problem from one side. The mismatch is defined by the pair.

Switch# show interfaces status

Switch# show interfaces GigabitEthernet0/2

  ...
     0 input errors, 0 CRC, 0 frame, 0 overrun, 0 ignored
     1240 output errors, 1187 collisions, 212 late collisions, 0 interface resets

a-full and a-1000 mean autonegotiated full duplex and 1 Gb/s. A plain half next to a peer that reports full is the mismatch. Cisco devices also log it when CDP notices:

%CDP-4-DUPLEX_MISMATCH: duplex mismatch discovered on GigabitEthernet0/2
 (not half duplex), with switch GigabitEthernet0/1 (half duplex).

On Linux, ethtool gives the same picture:

$ ethtool eth0
Settings for eth0:
    Supported link modes:   10baseT/Half 10baseT/Full
                            100baseT/Half 100baseT/Full
                            1000baseT/Full
    Speed: 1000Mb/s
    Duplex: Full
    Auto-negotiation: on
    Link detected: yes

If one end says Auto-negotiation: on with full duplex and the other says off with half, the settings are not the only problem — the forced end is not advertising at all.

6. Configuration Rules

The rule is simple and boring, which is why it is ignored:

  • Copper: leave both ends on auto. That is what autonegotiation is for, and it selects the highest common speed and full duplex.
  • If you must force, force both ends to the same speed and duplex. Document it, because the next engineer will assume auto.
  • Never mix one auto end with one forced-full end. This is the classic mismatch.
  • Do not force 1000BASE-T. Master/slave negotiation is required; leave it auto.
  • Change both parameters together. Setting speed without duplex, or duplex without speed, invites a half-configured port.
  • Expect a link bounce. Changing speed or duplex resets the link; verify both ends afterward.
interface GigabitEthernet0/1
 description Copper access port - auto/auto
 speed auto
 duplex auto
!
interface GigabitEthernet0/2
 description Provider handoff - fixed 100/full
 speed 100
 duplex full
# Linux: return to autonegotiation
sudo ethtool -s eth0 autoneg on

# Linux: force both parameters together
sudo ethtool -s eth0 speed 100 duplex full autoneg off

# Restart autonegotiation without changing settings
sudo ethtool -r eth0

Some platforms do not offer duplex auto as an explicit command; clearing the configuration or using the default is equivalent. The principle is the same: both ends must be in the same mode, and a forced end must match its peer exactly.

On Cisco platforms, speed nonegotiate disables negotiation on ports that support it, typically for fiber or DAC links where the peer is fixed. Do not apply it to copper access ports as a shortcut; it removes the mechanism that would have selected full duplex.

Auto-MDI/MDI-X is a separate convenience: copper ports detect whether the pair wiring is straight-through or crossover and adapt. Gigabit copper requires it, and it is why crossover cables are no longer part of the design. It does not negotiate speed or duplex.

8. Multi-Gig and Downshift

2.5GBASE-T and 5GBASE-T (NBASE-T) extend copper Ethernet beyond 1 Gb/s over cabling that cannot support 10GBASE-T. A multi-gig port advertises several speeds and selects the highest the link can sustain. A 10GBASE-T port on Cat5e may therefore come up at 2.5 or 5 Gb/s, or fail to reach 10G entirely; 10GBASE-T itself expects Cat6 for limited distances and Cat6a for full 100 m runs.

That is negotiation working, not a fault. Check three things before replacing hardware:

  • The negotiated speed on both ends (show interfaces, ethtool).
  • The cable category and length, including patch panels and couplers.
  • Whether the port and the NIC both support the higher rate.

Energy-Efficient Ethernet (802.3az) is sometimes mentioned alongside negotiation, but it is a separate low-power feature. It does not decide speed or duplex, and disabling it will not fix a mismatch.

9. Flow Control Is Not Duplex

Autonegotiation also advertises 802.3x pause capability, and the two ends can agree on symmetric or asymmetric pause. That agreement is independent of duplex. A link can be full duplex with pause enabled, full duplex with pause disabled, or half duplex where pause is effectively irrelevant.

Do not turn off autonegotiation to "fix" flow control, and do not read a pause setting as a duplex setting. On modern networks, pause is often disabled and congestion is handled by queueing and higher-layer protocols; the important part is that both ends agree on what they are doing.

10. Troubleshooting Workflow

  1. Check both ends. Record speed, duplex, and whether each is auto or forced.
  2. Compare the pair. Half on one side and full on the other is a mismatch; no link at all is usually a speed or signalling problem.
  3. Find the fixed end. Look for media converters, provider handoffs, old NICs, and templates that hard-code speed/duplex.
  4. Read the counters. Late collisions and runts on the half side; CRC/FCS errors on the full side.
  5. Check the physical path. Patch cords, couplers, cable length, and any hub in the run.
  6. Return both ends to auto on copper, bounce the link, and retest with a bulk transfer rather than a ping.
  7. If a peer is fixed and cannot be changed, match it exactly on your side — speed and duplex — and document the exception.
  8. Check logs and neighbours. CDP/LLDP mismatch messages often point directly at the port.

11. Three Cases

Case A: the provider handoff

A circuit was delivered through a media converter fixed at 100 Mb/s full duplex. The router port was left on auto.

EvidenceReading
Router port100 Mb/s, half duplex
Converter100 Mb/s, full duplex
CountersLate collisions climbing under load

The auto side had parallel-detected 100 Mb/s and assumed half duplex. Setting the router to 100/full matched the converter and cleared the late collisions. The provider could not enable autonegotiation, so a documented fixed setting was the correct exception.

Case B: the server that would not link at 1 Gb/s

A server NIC was hard-coded to 1000/full while the switch port remained on auto. The link flapped and sometimes stayed down.

EvidenceReading
Link stateDown or unstable
NIC settingSpeed 1000, duplex full, autoneg off
Switch settingAuto/auto

1000BASE-T needs autonegotiation to assign the clock master and slave. With one side forced, that exchange never happened. Returning both ends to auto produced a stable 1 Gb/s full-duplex link.

Case C: the 10G link that ran at 5G

A new 10GBASE-T switch port connected to a server over an existing Cat5e run. The link came up, but not at the expected rate.

EvidenceReading
Negotiated speed5 Gb/s on both ends
CableCat5e, long run with a coupler
ErrorsNone; stable link

Nothing was broken. The port and NIC supported multi-gig, the cable could not support 10G, and autonegotiation picked a rate that worked. The fix was a Cat6a run, not a duplex setting.

12. Exam Traps

  • "Autonegotiation always detects duplex." Only when both ends send FLPs. Parallel detection identifies speed and assumes half duplex.
  • "Auto/auto is the risky choice." On copper it is the recommended choice; mixed auto/forced is the risk.
  • "One side auto, one side forced full is fine if the speed matches." The auto side comes up half; that is a mismatch.
  • "Forced half plus auto is also a mismatch." The auto side assumes half, so they match — but it is still not best practice.
  • "1000BASE-T can run half duplex." 1000BASE-T is full duplex only.
  • "Gigabit autonegotiation only agrees speed." It also assigns master/slave and agrees duplex and pause.
  • "Fiber always autonegotiates." Many fiber and 10G links are fixed; 1000BASE-X negotiation is optional and does not change speed.
  • "Collisions on full duplex are normal under load." They indicate a duplex mismatch or faulty hardware.
  • "Late collisions mean congestion." They mean the collision arrived outside the slot-time window — usually a mismatch or an oversized collision domain.
  • "Speed and duplex are independent settings." They are negotiated together; configure them together.
  • "A crossover cable is required for gigabit." Auto-MDI/MDI-X handles it, and gigabit copper requires it.
  • "Flow control is part of duplex." Pause is advertised during negotiation but is a separate feature.

13. Key Takeaways

  • Speed and duplex are negotiated together; both ends must agree.
  • Autonegotiation exchanges FLP bursts carrying capabilities, pause, and next-page data.
  • Parallel detection recognises speed only, so an auto end facing a forced-full peer assumes half duplex.
  • A duplex mismatch links up but produces collisions, late collisions, CRC errors, and retransmissions.
  • Leave copper on auto/auto; if forced, force both ends and both parameters.
  • 1000BASE-T depends on autonegotiation for master/slave; do not hard-code it.
  • Fiber and DAC links may be fixed; verify the optic and the port rather than assuming negotiation.
  • Multi-gig downshift is a cable capability result, not a duplex fault.
