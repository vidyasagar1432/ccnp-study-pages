Copper Cables: UTP, STP, Straight-Through vs Crossover

Twisted pairs, shielding that actually works, category limits that matter, and which pin goes where — explained once, properly.

Cabling Copper Ethernet Pinouts Physical Layer

1. Copper Still Runs the Access Layer

Fibre owns the backbone, but copper owns the desk. It is cheap, it terminates in the field, it carries power as well as data, and it reaches 100 metres — enough for almost every floor plan. Even in a Wi-Fi-first design, the access points are usually copper-fed.

That longevity is exactly why copper faults are so common: the cable outlives several switch generations, gets moved, re-patched, stapled, kinked, and terminated by whoever was available that day. Understanding what is inside the jacket turns a category number into an engineering limit.

2. What Twisting Is Actually Doing

A twisted-pair cable is four pairs of insulated copper conductors, each pair twisted at a different rate, wrapped in a jacket. Each element exists for a reason:

  • The pair. Data travels as a differential signal: one wire carries the positive half, the other the negative. The receiver subtracts them, so noise that hits both wires equally cancels out.
  • The twist. It keeps the two wires in the same electromagnetic neighbourhood, so interference couples to both almost equally. Different twist rates per pair stop pairs from lining up and coupling into each other.
  • The jacket. Mechanical protection and, in plenum cable, fire safety.

This is why the last few centimetres matter so much. Untwisting a pair at the termination removes the cancellation exactly where the signal is weakest — at the connector. Standards limit untwisted length at the termination (commonly 13 mm / 0.5 in), and a sloppy punch-down is a measurable performance defect, not just an aesthetic one.

3. Categories and the Numbers That Matter

A category is a specification: a frequency range plus limits on loss, crosstalk, and return loss. The headline speed is the least useful part of the table.

Two limits catch people out:

  • Cat6 at 10G is a 55-metre cable, not a 100-metre cable. In a dense bundle, alien crosstalk can push the usable length lower. If you need 10GBASE-T across a full 100 m run, specify Cat6A.
  • The 100 m channel is 90 m permanent + 10 m of patch cords. Two 5 m cords, or one 10 m cord, not "90 m plus whatever is in the cabinet."

Also watch for copper-clad aluminium (CCA) and counterfeit cable. CCA has higher resistance than solid copper: it may pass a basic wiremap test, then fail under PoE load, overheat in bundles, or fall short of the category it claims. Buy certified cable and check the print on the jacket.

4. UTP, STP, and the Shielding Alphabet

The industry uses two naming systems, and vendors mix them. The ISO/IEC notation is the precise one: X/YTP, where X is the overall shield and Y is the per-pair shield.

Construction Common name Shielding When it helps
U/UTPUTPNoneOffices, normal EMI; the default
F/UTPFTP, ScTPOverall foil, unshielded pairsModerate EMI, reduced alien crosstalk
U/FTPSTP (some vendors)Foil per pair, no overall shieldHigh-frequency crosstalk control
S/FTPSTP, SFTPBraid overall + foil per pairIndustrial and high-EMI environments
SF/UTPSFTPBraid and foil overallHarsh electrical environments

Shielding is not free protection. A shield only works if it is bonded to ground at both ends through proper connectors, patch panels, and building ground. A floating shield is an antenna: it collects interference and couples it into the pairs it was meant to protect. That is why a badly installed STP link can perform worse than UTP.

Use shielded cable when the environment demands it — near variable-frequency drives, welding, radio transmitters, or where alien crosstalk between dense 10G bundles is the limiting factor. Then treat grounding as part of the cabling job, not an afterthought.

Solid conductors have lower loss but break when flexed repeatedly — never use a solid run as a patch cord. Stranded cords are flexible but lossier, which is why the patch-cord allowance in the 100 m budget is only 10 m.

6. Connectors and Pinouts

Ethernet over copper uses the 8P8C modular connector, universally called RJ45. Eight positions, four pairs. Two wiring standards define which colour lands on which pin: T568A and T568B.

Both standards are valid. Pick one for the site, document it, and use it everywhere; mixing them mid-run creates accidental crossovers. In the United States, T568B is the commercial default and T568A is common in government and older installations.

Note the 10/100 column: only pairs on pins 1-2 and 3-6 carry data. Gigabit and above use all four pairs, in both directions simultaneously. That asymmetry explains a classic fault: one broken pair still allows a gigabit port to fall back to 100 Mb/s instead of going down.

7. Straight-Through vs Crossover

The difference is not the cable — it is which standard is used on each end.

  • Straight-through: T568B on both ends (or T568A on both ends). Pin 1 goes to pin 1, all the way across. Used between devices whose transmit and receive pins are already opposite: PC to switch, router to switch, access point to switch.
  • Crossover: T568A on one end, T568B on the other. This swaps the transmit and receive pairs (1↔3 and 2↔6), so two devices with the same pin role can talk. Used between like devices: switch to switch, PC to PC, router to router.
  • Rollover: a different cable entirely, with pins reversed 1-8 to 8-1. It is not Ethernet; it is a Cisco console cable.

Left column: the wire that leaves pin n. Right column: where it lands.

Hold a crossover up to the light and the pairs appear to swap positions; hold a straight-through up and the colours match end to end. The diagram above is the electrical truth behind that.

Do you still need a crossover?

Most modern Ethernet ports implement Auto-MDIX, which detects the pairing and swaps transmit and receive internally. On gigabit and faster ports it is effectively universal, so a straight-through cable works between two switches. Crossover cables remain relevant for older 10/100 equipment, lab gear with Auto-MDIX disabled, and exam questions. Keep one in the bag; do not design around needing it.

The rollover cable is not interchangeable. It has a different purpose (console access), a different pinout (fully reversed), and usually a flat light-blue jacket. Plugging one into an Ethernet port does nothing useful.

8. PoE Changes the Cable Math

Power over Ethernet sends DC on the same pairs that carry data, which makes resistance a first-class concern:

  • Length costs voltage. A long run with thin conductors drops more volts, and the powered device sees less than the switch promised.
  • Heat is cumulative. Bundled cables cannot shed heat as well as a single run, so high-power PoE in dense bundles needs larger conductors (22 AWG) and attention to conduit fill.
  • CCA is disqualifying. Higher resistance means more loss and more heat exactly where PoE is least forgiving.
  • All four pairs matter for 802.3bt. Higher-power standards use the spare pairs, so a cable with a damaged pair may data-fail, power-fail, or both.

When a powered device reboots under load but idles fine, measure the delivered voltage and the cable length before replacing the device.

9. Installation Rules That Prevent Callbacks

  1. Respect bend radius. Roughly four times the cable diameter for installation; never a sharp kink.
  2. Do not over-tension. Pulling harder than the spec stretches pairs and changes their electrical properties.
  3. Keep twists to the termination. Untwist no more than the standard allows — about 13 mm.
  4. Separate from power. Run data and mains in separate pathways; cross at right angles when they must meet.
  5. Use the right jacket. CMP in plenum spaces, CMR in risers, outdoor-rated outside — not "whatever was on the reel."
  6. Support and dress. Velcro for bundles, service loops at panels, no cable hanging from a connector.
  7. Label both ends with the same identifier, and record the run in the documentation.
  8. Certify, don't just beep. A continuity tester proves copper is connected; a certifier proves the link meets its category.

10. Testing: Wiremap Is the Minimum

Test What it catches
WiremapOpens, shorts, reversed and crossed pairs, split pairs
Length / TDRRuns over the limit, breaks, and their distance from the tester
Insertion lossToo much attenuation — long runs, thin conductors, bad terminations
NEXT / PS NEXTCrosstalk between pairs and between cables
ACRFAttenuation-to-crosstalk ratio — the real usable bandwidth margin
Return lossImpedance mismatches from bad terminations or crushed cable
Propagation delay / skewPair-length differences that break high-speed signalling

Split pairs deserve a note. If pins 1-2 come from one pair and 3-6 from another, a basic continuity test still passes because every pin is connected. The link then suffers severe crosstalk and may only train at a lower speed. Only a wiremap that checks pair membership — or a certifier — catches it.

12. Two Short Case Studies

Case A: the gigabit port that would only do 100 Mb/s

A desk was moved and the new run linked at 100 Mb/s on a gigabit switch. Nothing was configured to limit speed.

Evidence Reading
Port statusUp, 100 Mb/s, full duplex
TDROpen on pair 4-5 at 3 m from the jack
InspectionOne conductor not seated in the punch-down block

Gigabit needs all four pairs; losing one leaves the port able to fall back to the two-pair 100BASE-TX mode. Re-terminating the jack restored 1 Gb/s. A link that "works but slowly" is often a partially working cable.

Case B: the 10G run that failed at 70 metres

A new 10GBASE-T link between two closets would not come up, while the same switch ports worked on a short lab cable.

Evidence Reading
CableCat6, 70 m permanent link
Limit10GBASE-T over Cat6 is specified to 55 m
EnvironmentBundled with 23 other active cables (alien crosstalk)

The cable was not defective; it was outside its specification for that speed. The fix was Cat6A (or fibre), not a new connector. Category is a distance-and-frequency contract, and 10G copper is where that contract is most often misunderstood.

show power inline Gi1/0/1 detail

For permanent links, a certifier report is worth more than any switch counter: it tells you which pair, which measurement, and how far down the run the defect sits.

14. Exam Traps

  • "Crossover cables connect like devices." Historically correct and still the expected exam answer, but Auto-MDIX makes it a non-issue on modern ports.
  • "Cat6 does 10G at 100 m." It does 10GBASE-T to 55 m. Cat6A is the 100 m copper answer.
  • "Cat7 is a TIA category." It is ISO/IEC Class F, with different connectors; TIA stops at Cat6A and Cat8 for twisted pair.
  • "Shielded cable is always better." Only with proper bonding at both ends; a floating shield can make things worse.
  • "T568A is wrong." Both T568A and T568B are valid; consistency matters, not the choice.
  • "Gigabit uses two pairs." 1000BASE-T uses all four. Only 10/100 uses two.
  • "A rollover cable is a crossover." It reverses all eight pins and is for console access, not Ethernet.
  • "The 100 m limit includes unlimited patch cords." It is 90 m permanent plus 10 m of cords.
  • "Solid cable is best everywhere." Solid for permanent runs; stranded for patch cords that flex.

15. Key Takeaways

  • Twisting cancels interference; untwisting at the termination throws that protection away.
  • The category number is a frequency and distance contract — Cat6 at 10G is 55 m, not 100 m.
  • Shielding only works when bonded at both ends; otherwise it is an antenna.
  • Straight-through is the same pinout at both ends; crossover is T568A on one end and T568B on the other.
  • Auto-MDIX made crossover cables situational, not obsolete in the exam or in legacy racks.
  • Copper carries power now, so resistance, gauge, and heat are part of the design.
  • A wiremap test is the minimum; certification is what proves a link meets its category.
