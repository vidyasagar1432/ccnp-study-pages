Connectors: RJ45, SFP, LC, SC — and What Each One Actually Is

Copper plugs, optical ferrules, and module cages are three different things. Confusing them makes cabling decisions, and cabling tickets, harder than they need to be.

Connectors RJ45 SFP LC / SC Fiber

1. Every Connector Is a Loss Event

A cable is only as good as its ends. Each connector adds insertion loss, each mating pair adds another, and each one is a mechanical interface that can be bent, scratched, contaminated, or plugged into the wrong thing. In a fibre budget, four connector pairs can eat most of a short-reach transceiver's margin.

The terminology does not help, because the industry uses one word for three different layers of the same link:

  • The medium's plug — RJ45 for twisted pair, LC or SC for fibre.
  • The module — SFP, SFP+, QSFP28; a transceiver that converts electrical signals to the medium.
  • The cage — the metal slot on the switch that holds the module.

"What connector does that port use?" has two correct answers: the cage takes an SFP, and the SFP takes an LC. Both are true; they describe different layers.

2. The Four You Will Actually Touch

Front views, not to scale. Ferrule diameters are the numbers that matter.

RJ45

Strictly speaking, the copper connector is 8P8C — eight positions, eight contacts. "RJ45" is the industry's everyday name, even though the original RJ45 registered jack was a different, keyed design. Everyone understands it; use it, but know the precise term.

  • Where: access ports, patch panels, keystone jacks, PoE devices.
  • Variants: unshielded and shielded plugs, solid and stranded conductors, Cat5e through Cat6A, field-terminable and factory-moulded.
  • Failure modes: broken latch tab, bent or retracted contacts, cracked housing, corrosion in damp environments, and the classic RJ11 (6P4C) plug forced into an RJ45 jack.
  • PoE note: the contacts carry current. Cheap plating and loose fits raise resistance, heat, and eventually intermittent links.

In industrial or outdoor settings, RJ45 is often replaced by M12 (D-coded for 100M, X-coded for 1G/10G) or a sealed IP67 RJ45. The electrical standard stays Ethernet; the mechanical interface changes.

LC

The 1.25 mm ferrule is the reason LC dominates data networking: half the diameter of SC means twice the port density, which is exactly what SFP cages need. The latch is small and must be pressed squarely; pulling on the fibre instead of the connector is how cables die.

  • Usually supplied as a duplex pair with a clip that keeps transmit and receive together.
  • Colour indicates polish: blue for UPC, green for APC; multimode versions are often beige or aqua.
  • Used in SFP, SFP+, SFP28, and most 10G–100G duplex optics.

SC

The 2.5 mm ferrule in a square, push-pull body. It is larger than LC but easier to handle, which is why it persists in PON/OLT ports, CATV gear, and older enterprise fibre panels. SC duplex clips exist, but single SC connectors are common in access networks.

ST and FC

ST (straight tip) uses a 2.5 mm ferrule with a bayonet twist-lock; it is legacy multimode plant. FC uses a 2.5 mm ferrule with a threaded coupling, common in telecom test gear and older single-mode installations. Neither belongs in a new data-centre build, but both appear in inherited infrastructure.

MPO / MTP

MPO is a multi-fibre connector using an MT ferrule: 8, 12, 16, or 24 fibres in one body, with guide pins for alignment. MTP is a brand name for an improved MPO, not a different standard. Used for 40G/100G/400G parallel optics and pre-terminated trunks.

  • Male connectors have pins; female connectors have holes. Two males cannot mate.
  • Key orientation and polarity method (A/B/C) must be consistent across the channel.
  • MPO-12 carries 40GBASE-SR4/100GBASE-SR4 using 8 of 12 fibres; MPO-16 is used for 400GBASE-SR8.

3. The SFP Family Is Not a Connector

An SFP is a hot-swappable transceiver in a standardized cage. The cage is soldered to the switch; the module slides in; the connector is on the module. That is why "SFP port" and "LC port" can both describe the same physical thing.

Three practical rules:

  1. Match the speed family. A 25G SFP28 will not link in a 10G-only SFP+ port, even though it fits.
  2. Respect coding. Many vendors lock modules to their own part numbers through the EEPROM. Third-party optics work when correctly coded; "unsupported SFP" is a coding message, not always a hardware fault.
  3. Do not pull by the fibre. Use the bail clasp or pull tab, and keep the dust cap on until the moment of insertion.

DAC and AOC assemblies put the transceiver and cable in one fixed unit. They are not connectors either, though they end in the same cage. Passive DAC is short and cheap; active DAC and AOC extend the reach.

Insertion loss is what dims the signal. Return loss is what reflects back toward the laser; poor return loss can destabilize a transmitter even when the received power looks acceptable. That is why APC exists: the angled end-face sends reflections into the cladding instead of back down the core.

5. Polarity: The Part Everyone Gets Wrong

  • Duplex LC: one strand transmits, one receives. If TX and RX are swapped at one end, the link stays down even though both sides see light.
  • MPO: the connector is keyed, and the fibre positions are mapped by a polarity method. A Type-A trunk is straight, a Type-B reverses, a Type-C crosses pairs.
  • Male/female: pins live on one side of every mated pair. Two pinned connectors will not mate; two unpinned ones will not align.
  • Breakouts: an MPO-to-4×LC harness turns eight fibres into four duplex links. The cassette's internal mapping is part of the polarity plan.

A polarity error often presents as "link down" or "link up but no receive traffic." Neither is fixed by cleaning, so check the mapping before you spend time on the end-faces.

7. Cleaning Is a Procedure, Not a Wipe

  1. Power down the transmitter before inspecting an end-face with a scope.
  2. Inspect at 200× or better. Look at the core zone first; a scratch there matters more than one on the cladding.
  3. Dry-clean with a one-click cleaner or a lint-free wipe. Most contamination comes off dry.
  4. Wet-clean only if needed with 99% isopropyl alcohol, then dry immediately with a lint-free wipe.
  5. Inspect again. If it is still contaminated, repeat with a fresh surface; never reuse a dirty swab.
  6. Clean the adapter as well, then cap both ends.

For copper, do not "clean" contacts with abrasives or file them. Replace a damaged patch cord; it costs less than the downtime. Use contact cleaner only on the metal contacts, never on fibre.

For a fibre link, the fastest useful test is not a full OTDR trace — it is the transceiver's receive power compared with the same link's baseline. A drop of 2 dB after a maintenance window points straight at a connector.

9. Two Short Case Studies

Case A: the 10G link that failed after a cabinet clean-up

A 10GBASE-SR link had run clean for a year. After a cleaning crew moved cabinets, it began reporting CRC errors during business hours.

Evidence Reading
Receive power−8.7 dBm, against a −5.2 dBm baseline
FECCorrected errors rising; uncorrected occasional
End-faceDust and a fingerprint on the LC at the patch panel

Cleaning restored −5.1 dBm. The connector had been uncapped while the cabinet was open, and the link's budget was thin enough that 3.5 dB of contamination mattered. Caps and inspection would have prevented the ticket.

Case B: the port that worked only when the cable was held

A desk lost connectivity intermittently. Re-seating the patch cord restored it for a few hours.

Patch cordLatch tab broken; the plug sat loose in the jack
PortNo damage; link flapped only under movement
CountersCRC errors and resets clustered around the interruptions

Replacing the cord fixed it. The lesson is cheap: patch cords are consumables, and a broken latch is not a repair job.

10. Design Rules for Connectors

  • Minimise mated pairs. Every adapter and extension is another loss event and another failure point.
  • Pick one fibre connector family per site. LC for density, SC where the installed plant demands it, MPO for parallel optics.
  • Standardise polish. UPC for data, APC where the carrier or PON requires it; never mix.
  • Plan polarity in writing. Record the method and the harness mapping before installation.
  • Label both ends with the same segment identifier, and record connector type and polish in the documentation.
  • Leave service loops and strain relief. A connector should never carry the cable's weight.
  • Cap everything that is not plugged in, including bulkheads and spare ports.
  • Budget the connectors. A 300 m OM4 link with four connector pairs has less than 1 dB of margin; count them before you add another panel.

11. Exam Traps

  • "SFP is a connector." It is a transceiver form factor and cage; the connector is usually LC.
  • "LC has a 2.5 mm ferrule." LC is 1.25 mm; SC, ST, and FC are 2.5 mm.
  • "SC is smaller than LC." The opposite: LC is the small form factor.
  • "APC and UPC are interchangeable." Different polish angles; mating them causes loss and possible damage.
  • "RJ45 is the official standard name." The connector is 8P8C; RJ45 is common usage.
  • "MPO always has 12 fibres." It comes in 8, 12, 16, and 24; 400GBASE-SR8 uses MPO-16.
  • "MTP and MPO are different standards." MTP is a brand of MPO.
  • "Any SFP fits any cage." It may fit physically and still not link because of speed or coding.
  • "A dirty connector only affects transmit." It attenuates whatever passes through it; the symptom usually shows up as low receive power.

12. Key Takeaways

  • A connector, a module, and a cage are three different things; "SFP port" describes the cage, not the plug.
  • RJ45/8P8C is the copper access connector; LC and SC are the fibre connectors you will meet most.
  • LC = 1.25 mm ferrule and high density; SC = 2.5 mm and easier handling.
  • Every mated pair costs loss and adds a failure point; count them against the budget.
  • Polish, polarity, and cleanliness cause most fibre connector faults.
  • Inspect, clean, and cap — in that order, every time.
  • Patch cords and optics are consumables; replace damaged ones rather than repairing them.
