MAC Addresses Explained: Structure, Types, and Where They Stop

Forty-eight bits, two special flags, and a scope that ends at the next router. Learn how MAC addresses are built, assigned, learned, spoofed, and troubleshot.

MAC Address Ethernet Layer 2 ARP

1. What a MAC Address Actually Is

A MAC address is a 48-bit identifier burned into a network interface — the burned-in address, or BIA. It is used by Ethernet to deliver a frame to a station on the local link. That is the whole job. It is not a name, not a location, and not a route. It identifies an interface at Layer 2, and it stops mattering the moment a router forwards the packet.

The address is 6 bytes, usually written as six hex pairs: 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E. It is case-insensitive. Cisco devices show it as three groups of four hex digits: 001a.2b3c.4d5e. Hyphens (00-1A-2B-3C-4D-5E) are common on Windows. The octets are the same; only the punctuation changes.

2. The Structure: OUI, Device, and Two Flags

The first 24 bits are the organizationally unique identifier (OUI), assigned by the IEEE to a vendor. The last 24 bits are assigned by that vendor to the individual interface. Software can override both, which is why the vendor prefix is a hint rather than a guarantee.

Two layers, two scopes: 48 bits at Layer 2, end-to-end at Layer 3.

Two bits inside the first octet change how the address is read:

  • I/G (individual/group), bit 0. 0 means unicast — one interface. 1 means multicast — a group. The broadcast address is the special case where every bit is 1.
  • U/L (universal/local), bit 1. 0 means globally administered by the IEEE. 1 means locally administered — an address assigned by software, a hypervisor, or an administrator.

This is why 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E is unicast and global (first octet 0x00), 01:00:5E:00:00:01 is multicast (0x01), and 02:00:00:00:00:01 is locally administered (0x02).

3. Unicast, Multicast, Broadcast

Type I/G bit Example Delivery
Unicast000:1A:2B:3C:4D:5EOne interface; the switch forwards to the learned port
Multicast101:00:5E:00:00:01A group; the switch floods unless it is snooping group membership
Broadcast1 (all bits)FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FFEvery interface in the broadcast domain (the VLAN)

Broadcast frames are not forwarded by routers. That is the boundary of the broadcast domain, and the reason a VLAN is a separate Layer 2 world. Multicast is subtler: a switch treats it like broadcast unless IGMP or MLD snooping tells it which ports actually asked for the group.

IPv4 multicast maps into Ethernet multicast with a fixed prefix: 01:00:5E plus the low 23 bits of the IP address. Because 5 bits are lost, 32 different IPv4 multicast groups share one MAC. IPv6 uses 33:33 plus the low 32 bits. The NIC filters in hardware, and a host may still receive frames for groups it does not care about.

4. Who Assigns Them

The IEEE Registration Authority assigns OUIs in blocks: 24-bit MA-L, 28-bit MA-M, and 36-bit MA-S. A vendor with an OUI can assign the remaining bits to its interfaces without asking anyone. The result is unique in theory. In practice:

  • Virtual machines get addresses from a hypervisor pool, not from a physical NIC.
  • Containers, bridges, and VPN interfaces generate addresses in software.
  • Phones and laptops randomise their MAC on Wi-Fi for privacy.
  • Administrators set addresses manually, sometimes to the same value twice.
  • Cheap hardware clones an OUI it does not own.

A locally administered address is not invalid. It is simply not guaranteed unique by the IEEE, and it may change. That distinction matters when a policy or a licence is tied to a MAC.

A NIC team shares one MAC across its member ports. The switch sees that address move between links, which is normal for active/standby and expected for LACP. It becomes a problem only when the switch's view and the host's teaming mode disagree — the classic cause of MAC flapping without a loop.

6. How Switches Use MAC Addresses

A switch builds a table of source MAC → port → VLAN. Every arriving frame refreshes the source entry. The destination lookup then chooses one of four outcomes: forward, filter, flood, or drop. Addresses age out after a few minutes of silence, typically 300 seconds.

  • Known unicast — forward out the learned port.
  • Unknown unicast — flood every other port in the VLAN.
  • Broadcast and multicast — flood every other port in the VLAN.
  • Destination on the ingress port — filter; do not send it back.

The table is per VLAN, so the same MAC can exist in two VLANs without a conflict. It maps addresses to ports, not to IP addresses. The IP-to-MAC relationship lives in ARP, on the hosts and routers, not in the switch.

7. MAC and IP: Two Different Scopes

An IP packet is addressed end to end. An Ethernet frame is addressed hop to hop. When a host sends to a destination on another subnet, it builds a frame to its default gateway's MAC and puts the far-end IP address inside. The router strips the frame, routes the packet, and builds a new frame for the next hop.

ARP is the glue inside one subnet: it asks "who has this IP?" by broadcast and learns the MAC from the reply. The ARP cache on the host and the MAC table on the switch are different databases with different keys. Confusing them is the source of many troubleshooting detours.

MAC addresses do not appear in the IP header, and routers do not forward frames by MAC. A MAC address is not routable, not globally meaningful, and not stable enough to be an identity.

These are protocol addresses, not host addresses. A switch may consume them itself (STP, LACP, LLDP) rather than forwarding them, which is why they can appear in a capture on one port and never reach the other.

9. Spoofing, Flooding, and Duplicates

Nothing in Ethernet proves that the source address in a frame belongs to the sender. A host can set any MAC it likes, and the switch will learn it. That fact drives three common problems:

  • Spoofing. An attacker impersonates a gateway or server MAC. Without 802.1X, port security, or MACsec, Layer 2 has no native authentication.
  • MAC flooding. An attacker sends frames with thousands of unique source addresses, filling the MAC table. The switch then floods unknown unicast everywhere, and the attacker can capture traffic.
  • Duplicate MACs. A cloned VM or a copied configuration produces two devices with one address. ARP replies conflict, the switch keeps moving the entry, and connectivity becomes intermittent.

Port security limits how many addresses a port may learn, can pin them as sticky entries, and can shut the port on violation. It is a mitigation, not authentication: a spoofed address that fits the policy is still accepted. MACsec (802.1AE) protects frame integrity and confidentiality but does not by itself prove the address is honest.

When you look up a MAC in an OUI database, remember what the answer means: it identifies the organization that owns the prefix, not the device, the user, or the location. For virtual and randomised addresses it often means nothing at all.

11. Three Cases

Case A: the cloned VM

Two application servers behind the same switch lost connections at random. Each worked alone; together, they interfered.

EvidenceReading
MAC tableThe same MAC alternating between two ports every few seconds
ARPTwo different IPs claiming the same MAC, with replies arriving from both
HistoryThe second VM was cloned from the first without regenerating the NIC address

Regenerating the MAC on the clone and clearing the ARP caches fixed it. Cloning a VM copies its configuration, including the address; the hypervisor does not always catch it.

Case B: the phone that would not stay on the Wi-Fi

A guest network used MAC filtering. One user's phone connected, dropped, and reconnected repeatedly, and the access point logs showed a new MAC each time.

EvidenceReading
Client MACDifferent on every association
First octetLocally administered address, generated by the OS
PolicyAllow-list keyed to the old hardware address

Privacy randomisation defeats MAC allow-lists by design. The fix is identity at a higher layer — 802.1X, a captive portal, or user-based policy — not a longer list of addresses.

Case C: the table that filled up

A floor's throughput collapsed and every port showed heavy unknown unicast flooding. Nothing had been re-cabled.

EvidenceReading
MAC tableAt capacity, entries changing constantly
SourceOne access port generating thousands of unique source MACs
EffectUnknown unicast flooded to every port; a capture saw traffic it should not have

Port security on the access edge, with a sensible maximum and a violation action, ended the flood and identified the port. MAC flooding is a Layer 2 denial-of-service and a reconnaissance technique at the same time.

12. Exam Traps

  • "A MAC address is 64 bits." It is 48 bits; IPv6 addresses are 128 and IPv4 are 32.
  • "MAC addresses are routable." They are local to a link and are rewritten at every router.
  • "The broadcast MAC is 00:00:00:00:00:00." Broadcast is FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF; all zeros is the null/unknown address.
  • "The OUI is 48 bits." The OUI is 24 bits; the remaining 24 identify the interface.
  • "The I/G bit is in the last octet." It is bit 0 of the first octet.
  • "The U/L bit is reserved and must be zero." It is 0 for global and 1 for locally administered addresses.
  • "A switch learns destination MACs." It learns source MACs and forwards on the destination.
  • "The MAC table maps MAC to IP." It maps MAC to port and VLAN; ARP maps IP to MAC.
  • "Changing a MAC requires new hardware." It is a software setting on most interfaces.
  • "Routers forward broadcast frames." They do not; broadcasts stop at the broadcast domain boundary.
  • "A MAC address uniquely identifies a person." It identifies an interface, and it can be changed or duplicated.

13. Key Takeaways

  • A MAC address is 48 bits: 24-bit OUI plus 24-bit interface identifier.
  • Bit 0 of the first octet is I/G; bit 1 is U/L.
  • Unicast, multicast, and broadcast are distinguished by the I/G bit and the all-ones address.
  • Switches learn source addresses and forward, filter, or flood based on destination and VLAN.
  • MAC addresses are local to a link; routers rewrite them at every hop.
  • ARP resolves IP to MAC; the switch's MAC table resolves MAC to port.
  • Virtual machines, teams, and privacy randomisation make MACs less unique than the standard assumes.
  • Spoofing is trivial without port security, 802.1X, or MACsec; a full MAC table causes flooding.
