LAN, WAN, MAN, and PAN: Network Scope Explained

Network types are usually classified by geographic scope. That single dimension predicts a surprising amount: who owns the equipment, which technologies are available, how much latency to expect, and what a megabit actually costs.

LAN WAN MAN PAN Fundamentals

1. Why Classify by Scope?

Scope is not just a label on a diagram. It determines four practical things:

  • Ownership — do you own the links, or do you rent them from a provider?
  • Technology — what media and protocols are practical at that distance?
  • Cost structure — a flat monthly fee, a per-megabit lease, or a capital build?
  • Performance envelope — latency, jitter, and loss behave very differently across a room versus across an ocean.

The common acronyms are PAN (Personal), LAN (Local), MAN (Metropolitan), and WAN (Wide). A few related terms appear in design work: WLAN (wireless LAN), WPAN (wireless PAN), CAN (campus area network), and SD-WAN (a way of building a WAN, not a new scope).

2. The Scope Spectrum at a Glance

Scope nests: a PAN rides on a LAN, a LAN connects to a MAN, and a MAN is one building block of a WAN. The ranges are typical, not definitions.

3. PAN — Personal Area Network

A PAN connects devices around one person. The defining feature is not distance but ownership and pairing: there is no network administrator, no provider, and usually no addressing hierarchy. Devices discover each other and associate directly.

  • Bluetooth and BLE — the canonical WPAN. A piconet has one master and up to seven active slaves; BLE is optimized for low power (sensors, wearables, beacons).
  • NFC — centimetre range, used for tap-to-pair and payments.
  • USB — a short wired PAN between a host and peripherals.
  • Zigbee and Thread — low-power mesh WPANs for building sensors and smart devices.
  • Personal hotspot / tethering — a phone acting as the gateway between a PAN and a WAN.

Design concerns in a PAN are battery, pairing security, and interference in the crowded 2.4 GHz band. Throughput is modest and latency is not tightly bounded, which is fine for its purpose.

4. LAN — Local Area Network

A LAN is a network under single administrative control, typically within a building or campus. This is where most enterprise engineering happens: switching, VLANs, wireless, and the first hop to everything else.

Characteristics

  • Owned infrastructure — you buy the switches, access points, and cabling.
  • High bandwidth, low latency — 1/10/25/40/100 Gbps is routine; sub-millisecond latency inside a building.
  • Broadcast domain at Layer 2 — ARP and DHCP rely on it; VLANs divide it.
  • Low cost per port — a switch port is cheap compared with a WAN circuit.
  • Technologies — Ethernet (802.3), Wi-Fi (802.11), often with PoE for phones, cameras, and APs.

Design notes

  • Use a hierarchy: access → distribution → core. Flat networks are easy to build and painful to troubleshoot.
  • Plan oversubscription deliberately; not every access port runs at line rate simultaneously.
  • Keep VLANs aligned to policy (users, voice, IoT, management) rather than to physical location alone.
  • Spanning tree still matters wherever there are redundant Layer 2 paths; consider routed access to avoid it.
  • Wireless is not "Ethernet without cables" — RF planning, roaming, and channel design are their own discipline.

5. MAN — Metropolitan Area Network

A MAN spans a city or metro region. In practice it is usually a provider-operated network that sells Ethernet or IP handoffs to many customers, or a municipality's own fiber ring.

  • Metro Ethernet — E-Line (point-to-point), E-LAN (multipoint), and E-Tree services delivered as VLANs or MPLS pseudowires.
  • Dark fiber — you lease strands and light them yourself; maximum control, maximum responsibility.
  • Carrier rings — fiber rings with protection switching, often using MPLS or optical transport.
  • Aggregation — broadband and mobile traffic from thousands of access nodes is collected and handed to core networks.

Latency across a MAN is typically single-digit milliseconds, and the provider may offer protection (sub-50 ms failover) rather than a hard latency SLA. The important mental model: a MAN is a shared infrastructure that behaves like a very large LAN from the customer's perspective, but whose failure domains you do not control.

6. WAN — Wide Area Network

A WAN connects sites across cities, countries, or continents. The defining feature is that it crosses provider boundaries, so you rent capacity and depend on someone else's operations.

  • Leased lines — dedicated, predictable, expensive per megabit.
  • MPLS L3VPN — provider-managed private routing with QoS and SLAs; still widespread in enterprise and carrier networks.
  • Internet / DIA — cheap and ubiquitous, but best effort; usually paired with overlays and encryption.
  • SD-WAN — an overlay that steers traffic across multiple underlays (MPLS, broadband, LTE/5G) based on policy and path quality.
  • Cellular and satellite — LTE/5G for backup or remote sites; GEO satellite RTT is roughly 500–700 ms, LEO is far lower but still tens of milliseconds.
  • Subsea cables — the physical backbone of intercontinental connectivity.

WAN realities

  • Latency is physics plus path. Distance sets the floor; circuitous routing and queueing add the rest.
  • Cost is per megabit, not per port. WAN capacity is the budget line that shapes designs.
  • Loss and jitter are normal. Applications must tolerate them or the design must compensate (QoS, FEC, caching, local breakout).
  • MTU varies. PPPoE, IPsec, and cellular links can be smaller than 1500 bytes — a classic source of "works small, fails large" bugs.
  • Routing is often BGP. At the edge you speak BGP to the provider; inside you may run an IGP.

8. Where the Boundaries Blur

Real networks do not respect the acronyms, and exam questions sometimes exploit that.

  • Campus vs MAN. A university spanning a city may call its network a campus LAN while it physically behaves like a MAN. Ownership is the better test than distance.
  • MAN vs WAN. Metro Ethernet sold as a "WAN service" is common. If the provider hands you an Ethernet port and you run your own routing over it, the scope label matters less than the SLA.
  • SD-WAN is not a scope. It is an architecture that runs over any combination of WAN underlays.
  • Cloud extends the LAN. VPCs and cloud interconnects look like someone else's data center attached to your WAN — with their own MTU, routing, and security models.
  • VLAN is not a LAN. A VLAN is a logical broadcast domain inside switched infrastructure; a LAN is a scope. You can have many VLANs in one LAN, and one VLAN stretched across a MAN.
  • WLAN is a LAN. "Wireless LAN" is still a LAN in scope and ownership; only the medium changes.

9. Choosing by Scope: Practical Guidance

Requirement Best fit Reasoning
Connect a headset to a laptop PAN No infrastructure, pairing is the whole setup
Connect 500 users on one floor LAN Owned switching, high bandwidth, low latency
Connect three buildings across a city MAN or Metro Ethernet Provider fiber avoids building your own ring
Connect offices in two countries WAN (MPLS, SD-WAN, or internet + overlay) Crosses provider boundaries; needs routing and SLA
Add a temporary site quickly WAN over 4G/5G Fastest to deploy; watch latency, MTU, and data caps
Segment finance from engineering LAN design (VLANs, ACLs, firewalls) Policy problem, not a scope problem

10. Exam Traps

  • "A LAN must be Ethernet." No — Wi-Fi LANs are still LANs. The scope is about ownership and locality, not the medium.
  • "A WAN always means the internet." No — MPLS VPNs and leased lines are WANs that never touch the public internet.
  • "MAN is obsolete." No — metro Ethernet and dark fiber are how most enterprises reach their providers.
  • "PAN requires a router." No — pairing is direct; a phone hotspot is a PAN-to-WAN gateway, not a requirement for the PAN itself.
  • "VLAN = LAN = subnet." These are three different things: a VLAN is a Layer 2 broadcast domain, a subnet is a Layer 3 construct, and a LAN is a scope of ownership.
  • "Latency is a WAN problem only." LAN microbursts and queueing cause real latency too; it is just smaller and easier to bound.

11. Key Takeaways

  • PAN is personal, paired, and provider-free — Bluetooth, BLE, NFC, USB.
  • LAN is organization-owned, high-bandwidth, low-latency — Ethernet, Wi-Fi, VLANs.
  • MAN is city-scale and usually provider-operated — metro Ethernet, dark fiber, carrier rings.
  • WAN crosses provider boundaries — MPLS, SD-WAN, internet, cellular, satellite.
  • Ownership and SLA matter more than the distance label when you are designing.
  • Cost and latency scale with scope; so do failure domains and the number of parties you depend on.
