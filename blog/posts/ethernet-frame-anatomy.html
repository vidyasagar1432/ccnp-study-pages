Anatomy of an Ethernet Frame: A Byte-by-Byte Dissection

Fourteen bytes of header, a payload, some padding, and a CRC. Here is every octet of a real 64-byte ARP request, and the rules that decide how it is built.

Ethernet Frames Layer 2 FCS

1. Frame, Packet, Segment

The words are not interchangeable. A segment is TCP's unit of data. A packet is IP's unit. A frame is Ethernet's unit — the envelope that carries the IP packet across one link. When the packet crosses a router, the frame is discarded and a new one is built for the next hop; the IP packet inside is unchanged (apart from its TTL).

Ethernet's frame is deliberately simple. It has no version field, no options, no fragmentation support, and no length field in its modern form. Everything that needs those features lives in the payload.

Offsets shift by 4 bytes once an 802.1Q tag is inserted, and by 8 with two tags. That is the only place the header grows.

3. The Preamble and SFD: Eight Bytes You Never Capture

Before the destination address, the transmitter sends seven bytes of 10101010 (0xAA) and one byte of 10101011 (0xAB). The alternating pattern lets the receiver's clock recover the transmitter's timing; the final two consecutive 1 bits mark the boundary where the frame begins.

These eight bytes exist only on the wire. Network cards strip them before handing the frame to the operating system, which is why a packet capture starts at the destination MAC. They are also excluded from the 64-byte minimum frame size — a point exam questions like to test.

4. Destination and Source MAC

Six bytes each, destination first. The order matters at the hardware level: a switch can start its table lookup as the first bytes arrive, without waiting for the source address.

In the example below, the destination is FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF — broadcast — and the source is 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E. The first octet of the source contains the I/G and U/L bits; the upper 24 bits are the vendor's OUI.

Switches learn from the source and forward on the destination. They do not care what the addresses mean, only where they have been seen. That is why a duplicated or spoofed MAC can confuse a whole VLAN.

5. Type or Length: The Two-Dialect Header

The two bytes after the source address are read one of two ways:

  • Ethernet II: a value of 0x0600 (1536) or greater is an EtherType, naming the protocol in the payload. 0x0800 is IPv4, 0x0806 is ARP, 0x86DD is IPv6.
  • IEEE 802.3: a value of 1500 or less is a length, and the payload begins with an LLC header. Values 1501–1535 are undefined.

Both dialects share the same wire format up to this point, which is why they can coexist. In practice, Ethernet II won: it names the upper protocol directly, without the extra LLC/SNAP layer.

6. Payload, MTU, and Padding

The payload is the IP packet or ARP message. Its maximum is the MTU, 1500 bytes by default. Its minimum is 46 bytes. If the upper layer supplies less, Ethernet pads the frame to reach the 64-byte minimum, and the pad is not distinguished from data on the wire.

How does the receiver know where the real payload ends? The upper protocol tells it. IPv4 has a total-length field; ARP has fixed hardware and protocol address lengths. Ethernet itself does not carry a length in Ethernet II, so the pad is simply ignored by the layer above.

Our example ARP message is 28 bytes, so 18 bytes of padding are added. That is the entire reason the frame below is 64 bytes long.

7. The FCS: Four Bytes That Decide Fate

The frame check sequence is a 32-bit CRC computed over the destination MAC through the end of the payload and padding. It does not cover the preamble, the SFD, or the interframe gap. The polynomial is the standard CRC-32 (0x04C11DB7, reflected 0xEDB88320), with an all-ones initial value and a final complement.

The transmitter appends the result least-significant byte first. The receiver recomputes the CRC over the received bytes; if the result does not match, the frame is dropped and a CRC/FCS error counter increments. There is no negative acknowledgement and no retransmission. Reliability, if it is needed, comes from TCP or the application.

You can verify the FCS of any frame yourself:

import zlib, struct

frame = bytes.fromhex("...")   # DA through padding, no FCS
crc = zlib.crc32(frame) & 0xffffffff
print(hex(crc))                # 0xa81f0314
print(struct.pack("<I", crc).hex())  # 14031fa8, on the wire

Most NICs strip the FCS before delivery, so Wireshark usually does not show it. If a capture includes it, the four bytes appear after the payload, and a bad FCS is reported as a malformed frame.

8. The Interframe Gap

After the FCS, the link goes quiet for at least 96 bit times — 12 byte times. At 10 Mb/s that is 9.6 µs; at 100 Mb/s, 0.96 µs; at 1 Gb/s, 96 ns; at 10 Gb/s, 9.6 ns. The gap gives the receiver time to prepare for the next preamble and prevents one frame from running into the next.

Like the preamble, the IFG is not part of the frame and does not appear in captures. It still consumes wire time: a minimum 64-byte frame actually occupies 8 + 64 + 12 = 84 byte times on the link.

Walking through it:

10. Ethernet II vs IEEE 802.3

The Type/Length fork creates two frame dialects that share the same header layout:

Ethernet II (DIX) IEEE 802.3
Bytes 12–13EtherType (≥ 0x0600)Length (≤ 1500)
Payload starts withThe network protocol directlyLLC header: DSAP, SSAP, control
To carry IPEtherType 0x0800LLC/SNAP: AA AA 03 00 00 00 08 00
Typical use todayEssentially all trafficLegacy, some industrial and control protocols

LLC/SNAP adds eight bytes before the IP header, which is eight fewer bytes of payload — and one more header to parse. Ethernet II avoided both, which is most of the reason it became the default. A switch forwards either without caring; a protocol analyser has to look at the value to know which one it is seeing.

11. VLAN Tags and QinQ

An 802.1Q tag is inserted immediately after the source MAC, shifting everything else by four bytes:

Part Size Contents
TPID2 bytes0x8100 for 802.1Q; 0x88A8 for QinQ outer tag
PCP3 bitsPriority 0–7 for QoS
DEI1 bitDrop eligible indicator
VID12 bitsVLAN ID 1–4094 (0 = priority tag, 4095 reserved)

A tagged frame's EtherType now sits at offset 0x10 instead of 0x0C. Maximum frame size grows to 1522 bytes for one tag and 1526 for two. The payload MTU does not change: 1500 bytes is still 1500 bytes of IP.

QinQ (802.1ad) stacks a second tag for service-provider transport, usually with an outer TPID of 0x88A8. A switch that does not understand the outer tag may treat the frame as a giant or drop it, which is why MTU and tag awareness must match end to end.

12. Frame Size Limits

Frame Payload Size Note
Minimum0–46 bytes64 bytesPadding fills the payload to 46
Standard1500 bytes1518 bytesThe default Ethernet MTU
Tagged1500 bytes1522 bytesOne 802.1Q tag
QinQ1500 bytes1526 bytesTwo tags
Jumbo9000 bytes9018 bytesVendor-defined, not an IEEE standard

Names for the out-of-range cases are worth knowing because they appear in interface counters:

  • Runt — shorter than 64 bytes; often a collision fragment or a truncated frame.
  • Giant — longer than the allowed maximum; often an MTU or jumbo mismatch.
  • Jabber — oversized with a bad FCS; usually a failing NIC or transceiver.
  • Alignment error — the frame ends on a non-octet boundary; a physical-layer or duplex problem.

Jumbo frames are a convention, not a standard. If every device in the path supports 9000-byte MTUs, they reduce per-packet overhead; if one device does not, the result is silent black-holing of large packets. Test the whole path before enabling them.

14. Reading Frames in a Capture

Packet captures are where frame anatomy becomes practical, provided you know what the capture point did to the frame:

  • The FCS is usually absent. The NIC verifies and strips it. Wireshark's "Frame check sequence" field appears only when the capture includes it.
  • The preamble is always absent. Captures begin at the destination MAC.
  • Tags depend on the capture point. Capturing on a switch port may show untagged frames on an access port and tagged frames on a trunk.
  • Offloads distort the picture. Large send offload can show 64 KB "frames" that never existed on the wire; checksum offload can mark outbound packets as having bad checksums.
  • Truncated captures lie. A snaplen shorter than the frame shows a runt that the wire never carried.
# Capture with link-layer headers and full frames
tcpdump -i eth0 -e -XX -s 0

# Show only Ethernet headers
tcpdump -i eth0 -e -c 10

# Disable offloads before capturing on the sending host
sudo ethtool -K eth0 tso off gso off gro off

# Wireshark display filters
eth.type == 0x0806
vlan.id == 100
eth.dst == ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff
frame.len < 64

One practical habit: when a capture looks impossible — a 9000-byte frame on a 1500-byte path, or a checksum error on every outbound packet — suspect the capture point or an offload before suspecting the network.

15. Exam Traps

  • "The preamble is part of the 64-byte frame." It is not; the 64 bytes run from destination MAC to FCS.
  • "The FCS covers the preamble." It covers destination MAC through payload and padding only.
  • "The Type field is always an EtherType." In 802.3 it is a length; values 1501–1535 are undefined.
  • "A 1500-byte payload makes a 1500-byte frame." It makes 1518 bytes; add 4 per tag.
  • "VLAN tagging increases the MTU." The payload MTU stays 1500; the frame grows to 1522.
  • "A switch always checks the FCS before forwarding." Store-and-forward does; cut-through may not.
  • "A bad FCS triggers a retransmission." The frame is dropped silently; upper layers decide.
  • "46 bytes is the minimum frame size." 46 is the minimum payload; the frame minimum is 64.
  • "Jumbo frames are defined by IEEE 802.3." They are not; 9000-byte MTUs are a vendor convention.
  • "QinQ always uses 0x8100 twice." The outer tag is commonly 0x88A8.

16. Key Takeaways

  • A frame is a 14-byte header, a 46–1500-byte payload, and a 4-byte FCS, plus padding when needed.
  • The preamble and SFD add 8 bytes on the wire; the interframe gap adds 12 byte times of silence.
  • Destination comes first so switches can look up before the frame finishes arriving.
  • Type versus length is the fork between Ethernet II and IEEE 802.3; Ethernet II is the modern default.
  • Padding exists for collision detection, not for the upper protocol; the receiver ignores it using the payload's own length.
  • The FCS is a CRC-32 over the frame, checked and stripped by the NIC; failure means silent discard.
  • An 802.1Q tag inserts 4 bytes after the source MAC, shifting the EtherType and raising the maximum to 1522.
  • 64/1518/1522 are the numbers to know; runt, giant, and jabber are what happens outside them.
