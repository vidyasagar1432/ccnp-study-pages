Ethernet Standards and Speeds: From 10BASE-T to 800G

Decode any Ethernet name, follow the speed ladder, and understand why lanes, encodings, and duplex rules changed along the way.

Ethernet 802.3 Speeds Optics Standards

1. Ethernet Is a Family, Not a Speed

"Ethernet" describes a set of IEEE 802.3 standards covering the frame format, the MAC rules, and dozens of physical layers. The same Ethernet frame that crossed a coaxial bus in 1983 crosses a 400G fibre today. The framing survived; the physics underneath was replaced several times.

That is why an Ethernet name is so dense: it encodes the speed, the signalling scheme, the medium, and sometimes the number of lanes. Once you can read one name, you can read all of them.

2. Decoding the Name

Take 100GBASE-LR4. It is four facts in one string.

The suffix is not decoration: it tells you the medium, the reach, and how many lanes or wavelengths are in play.

  • Speed — 10G, 40G, 100G. Sometimes written as 10G, sometimes 10GBASE.
  • BASE — baseband signalling. Every modern Ethernet variant is baseband; BROAD belonged to early broadband coax systems.
  • Reach/medium code — T twisted pair, S short wavelength (850 nm), L long wavelength (1310 nm), E extended (1550 nm), CR copper cable/DAC, K backplane.
  • Lane count — 4 means four lanes or four wavelengths. For SR4 those are parallel fibres; for LR4 they are four CWDM wavelengths over one fibre pair. Same number, very different cabling.

Two suffixes that confuse people: LRM is long-reach multimode (10GBASE-LRM, 220 m on OM1/OM2 with electronic dispersion compensation), and CX4 was a short-lived 10G copper interface that predates SFP+ DACs.

The ladder is not a straight line. 40G arrived before 25G, and 25G then became more popular than 40G because one 25G lane maps cleanly to a 100G port and a server NIC. 50G per lane followed, then 100G per lane. Each step is really a decision about lane rate.

4. Line Rate Is Not Payload Rate

Encodings add overhead, so the clock on the wire runs slightly faster than the advertised rate.

Speed Encoding Line rate Why

This is why a 25G port reports 25.78125 Gbaud on the line but 25 Gb/s of Ethernet payload, and why high-speed links expose FEC counters. FEC is not a fault; uncorrectable FEC is.

5. How Duplex Disappeared

Early Ethernet shared a coaxial segment. Every station heard every frame, collisions were normal, and CSMA/CD decided who could transmit. A hub extended that model: one collision domain, half duplex everywhere.

The switch changed the model. A dedicated port per device means the medium is not shared, so transmit and receive can happen at once and collisions cannot occur. Full duplex became the default, and CSMA/CD became a museum piece.

  • 10/100/1000BASE-T still support half duplex for compatibility, which is why autonegotiation matters.
  • 10GBASE-T and every optical standard above 1G are full duplex only.
  • Half duplex at 1G is rare and almost always a misconfiguration, not a design choice.

The practical consequence: a duplex mismatch produces late collisions and CRC errors that look like a cable fault but are really an autonegotiation failure.

6. Autonegotiation and Speed Mismatch

Twisted-pair Ethernet negotiates speed and duplex over the link itself. Fibre generally does not: the optic defines the speed, and two different line rates simply will not link.

Situation Result
Both sides auto, both support 1G/2.5GHighest common speed, full duplex
One side forced, one side auto (copper)Speed may match; duplex usually does not → mismatch
1G SFP in a 10G SFP+ portOften works; the port downshifts if it supports 1G
10G SFP+ in a 1G SFP portNo link — the port cannot run the line rate
25G SFP28 in a 10G SFP+ portNo link unless the port is explicitly 25G-capable
2.5G device on a 1G-only portLinks at 1G; the higher rate is never advertised

NBASE-T (2.5G/5G) is the exception that made autonegotiation interesting again: it reuses Cat5e/Cat6 cabling and negotiates down if the cable cannot support the higher rate. That is why an access point rated 2.5G may link at 1G after a cable upgrade is skipped.

DAC (direct-attach copper) and AOC (active optical cable) are assemblies, not separate 802.3 PHYs: they package a standard electrical interface into a fixed cable. Passive DAC is short and cheap; AOC goes further and is thinner.

Ports are usually backward-compatible within a family, not across families. An SFP+ port may take an SFP; a QSFP28 port may take a QSFP+; but a QSFP28 optic will not fit an SFP+ cage. Check the port's supported speed list before ordering.

9. Frame Size: The Numbers People Mix Up

  • Minimum frame: 64 bytes including FCS. Shorter frames are runts.
  • Standard maximum: 1518 bytes — 14-byte header, 1500-byte payload, 4-byte FCS.
  • With an 802.1Q tag: 1522 bytes. This is still standard Ethernet, not "jumbo."
  • Jumbo frames: typically 9000 bytes, vendor-defined, and not part of 802.3. Every device in the path must agree, or large packets vanish silently.
  • MTU is a Layer 3 concept. The interface MTU usually refers to the IP payload; the Ethernet frame adds 14 bytes of header and 4 of FCS (plus tags).

A jumbo-frame mismatch is a classic "ping works, large transfers hang" fault, and it is easy to misdiagnose as an application problem.

10. PoE Is Part of the 802.3 Family

These are Ethernet standards in the literal sense — amendments to 802.3 — but they change the power budget, not the frame. 802.3bt uses all four pairs, which is why cable gauge and bundle heating matter more at the top of the table.

Do not buy a speed because it is on the ladder. Buy the lowest speed that covers the next three to five years of demand, then spend the difference on redundancy and fibre that will still be there when the switches are replaced.

12. Two Short Case Studies

Case A: the 2.5G access point that linked at 1G

A Wi-Fi 6E access point was installed on a switch advertised as "multi-gigabit." It associated clients fine, but throughput never exceeded about 940 Mb/s.

Evidence Reading
Port statusUp at 1000 Mb/s full duplex
Port capability1G only on that model; the "multi-gig" claim applied to uplinks
CableCat5e, 40 m — capable of 2.5G

The link was healthy and the cable was fine; the port could not advertise 2.5G. Moving the AP to a 2.5G port raised the ceiling. "Works" and "works at the intended speed" are different acceptance tests.

Case B: the 40G optic that would not break out

A 40G port was meant to serve four 10G servers through a breakout harness. It came up as a single 40G link instead, and the servers saw nothing.

Optic40GBASE-LR4 — four wavelengths over one duplex LC pair
HarnessMPO-to-4×LC, expecting four physical lanes
ResultNo per-lane light; breakout impossible with this optic

LR4 multiplexes four wavelengths onto two fibres; SR4 uses eight fibres, four in each direction, and can be broken out. The fix was a 40GBASE-SR4 optic with the correct harness, not a configuration change.

13. Exam Traps

  • "Ethernet is 10/100/1000." It runs to 800G and beyond; the IEEE 802.3 family is much larger.
  • "10GBASE-T runs 100 m on Cat6." 55 m on Cat6, 100 m on Cat6A.
  • "40GBASE-LR4 can break out to 4 × 10G." No — four wavelengths over one pair. SR4 can break out; LR4 cannot.
  • "Half duplex is available at 10G." No; 10G and above are full duplex only.
  • "Fibre autonegotiates speed." The optic sets the rate; mismatched line rates do not link.
  • "Jumbo frames are an Ethernet standard." They are vendor-defined; 1518 (1522 with a tag) is standard.
  • "An SFP+ port takes any SFP28 optic." Different speed families; check port capability.
  • "100GBASE-SR4 uses 12 fibres." It uses 8 of the 12 in an MPO-12 connector.
  • "2.5G requires Cat6." 2.5GBASE-T is specified over Cat5e to 100 m.
  • "802.3 is Wi-Fi." That is 802.11. 802.3 is Ethernet.

14. Key Takeaways

  • An Ethernet name encodes speed, baseband signalling, medium/reach, and lane count.
  • The frame format has survived every speed change; the physical layer has been replaced repeatedly.
  • Lane rate drives port economics: 25G became dominant because it maps cleanly into 100G.
  • Line rate is slightly higher than payload rate because of encoding overhead.
  • Switching ended collisions; half duplex survives only as a compatibility mode below 10G.
  • Autonegotiation is a copper feature; fibre speed is defined by the optic.
  • Port capability, not just the standard, decides what actually links.
